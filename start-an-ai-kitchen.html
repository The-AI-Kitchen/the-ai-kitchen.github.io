01 / Gather your ingredients

Make room for beginners.
Give everyone something to do.

1

A welcoming invitation

No application and no coding prerequisite. Invite people across majors and job roles around a useful question: planning a trip, exploring feedback, making a game, or preparing a learning activity. State what people will try and what they need to bring.

2

A guest with a real workflow

Invite someone willing to show their work, including the choices and wrong turns. Industry guests, faculty, staff, and students bring different perspectives. Where useful, show AI using tools to create, inspect, and revise a file or small app. Participants can try a smaller step with the access they have. Choose one task and one reusable lesson; share our guest chef guide to prepare.

3

Student hosts with time to prepare

Students help choose the exercise, test it beforehand, and get participants unstuck. Budget for paid preparation and hosting, with clear hours, supervision, and someone to turn to for help. Begin with a session small enough for your hosts to support.

4

Faculty and staff learning, too

Find a campus partner who can help with the room, tools, and continuity. Invite advisers, teachers, researchers, and artists to bring their expertise and questions. Everyone can contribute knowledge while learning alongside others.

An invitation you can adapt

“Curious about AI? Come watch someone use it for a real task, then try one small step with help nearby. Students, faculty, and staff from every field are welcome. No coding experience needed.”

Add your task, date, room, tool-access plan, and contact for questions or access needs.

02 / Your first Taste Test

Show. Try. Compare.

Adaptable sample
90 minutes

A compact first session could look like this. Adjust the pace for your audience, exercise, and available support. SCU’s Friday format leaves more time for the demonstration and open projects.

  1. 0–10 min

    Welcome & get ready

    Explain the task in plain language. Help people open the tool and practice material. Invite them to work with a partner if that helps.

  2. 10–35 min

    Watch one real workflow

    The guest works through a specific problem on screen. Ask them to explain what they give the AI, what they inspect, and how they decide what to change.

  3. 35–65 min

    Try one small step

    Participants repeat a manageable part using their own example or shared practice material. Hosts circulate. Offer one optional extension for people ready to go further.

  4. 65–80 min

    Compare what happened

    Invite a few people to show a result or a sticking point. Ask: What did you notice? What would you check? What would you change next?

  5. 80–90 min

    Make a connection

    Invite brief project questions and introductions. Name the next opportunity to meet, and share the exercise and useful links afterward.

A small exercise to borrow

Choose a movie for two people.

Pair up, or use two fictional profiles. Open a browser AI tool your campus provides. The goal: three movies both people would enjoy tonight.

“Interview us one person at a time about our preferences, constraints, films we’ve seen, time available, and where we can watch. Ask one question and wait for the answer. Then suggest three films we might both enjoy, explain the fit for each person, and flag assumptions to check.”

Leave with: a three-film shortlist, reasons each fits both people, and one refinement. Check availability yourself; verify any streaming claims before choosing.

04 / Stock the kitchen

Plan for people, time, and access.

A dedicated space can come later. Build your local budget around the work required to make a modest first session welcoming and workable.

Resource and budget categories for your local estimate
ResourceWhat to plan for
People & preparationStudent host pay for preparation, the session, and follow-up; staff or faculty coordination; guest preparation and any agreed honorarium or travel.
Tool accessStart with tools your institution already provides, or a free option that supports the whole exercise. Budget for temporary access or credits when needed. Test limits before the session and explain any participant costs in advance.
Room & equipmentA borrowed classroom or meeting room, reliable Wi-Fi, a projector or shared screen, power, and a plan for participants who need a device.
Participation supportAccessible materials, captions, accommodations, clear directions, and optional refreshments. Include recording or editing time if you choose to share a recording with permission.
ContinuityA page for the schedule and materials, reminders, host debriefs, and time for introductions and project follow-up.

Match the exercise to the access. A guest can show an advanced workflow while participants try a smaller step in a browser tool. See our tools guide for examples, and check what your campus supports.

05 / Keep cooking

Repeat first. Grow with your community.

Run another small session and improve one thing. Invite a different field into the room. Teaching, art, research, advising, and everyday life can all offer useful questions.

Make space for people with ongoing projects to ask for guidance. Introduce a student builder to a faculty or staff member who knows the subject, or connect people with complementary skills. Agree on a manageable next step and who will follow up.

When there is interest and enough support, add longer projects, mentoring, or a showcase. Our joint Demo Night with CSEN 174 is one example of a later opportunity to share work.

Notice who comes, who gets a chance to try, and what gets in the way. Use that feedback to improve the experience; attendance alone does not tell you what people learned.
